# How long a custom build actually takes

If you are planning backwards from an event date, the number you need is not how long
fabrication takes. It is how long everything before fabrication takes, because that is where
schedules actually fail.

A custom environment has four phases, and only one of them is the build.

## 1. Design and approval

Longer than anyone budgets, because it is not a production constraint, it is a decision
constraint. The drawing waits on your side: a stakeholder who has not seen it, a venue walk
that has not happened, a brand approval that needs a round.

The useful thing to know is that this phase is almost entirely within your control, and it is
usually the largest single block of time. A week saved here is worth more than a week saved
anywhere else, because it is the only week that costs nothing to save.

## 2. Sourcing

This is where the schedule meets reality. Material has to exist, in the quantity you need, in
the finish specified, and it has to ship.

For soft goods that means fabric availability, and fabric is the most common cause of a
redesign late in the process. A discontinued run or a dye lot that will not repeat can change
the drawing after the drawing was approved.

For anything going into a venue, it also means fire certification, and this is the part most
schedules get wrong. Treatment vendors frequently quote a **duration** rather than a **date**:
"ten days from receipt," not "ready on the twentieth." You cannot build a calendar backwards
from a duration. Until the material is physically with the treater, the finish date is an
estimate, not a commitment.

Certification also has to match the exact material and process used, which means it cannot be
sorted out generically in advance.

## 3. Fabrication

The most predictable phase, and the one everyone worries about most.

It is predictable because it is internal: shop capacity, hours, and a known process. If a
company owns its shop, it can tell you the real number and hold it. If it is brokering the
build, its lead time is somebody else's queue position, which is why brokered schedules move
without warning.

## 4. Install

Constrained by the venue, not by you or the builder. Load-in windows, dock access, freight
elevators, union rules, the other event in the room the day before. The build can be finished
and still wait a week because the room is not available.

## The honest way to ask

Do not ask how long it takes. Ask **what has to be true before fabrication can start**, and
**which of those things is outside your control**.

A real answer includes dependencies. Material availability, treatment turnaround, venue access.
A lead time with no dependencies in it is a sales number, and it will move.

## Building in float

Put the buffer where the uncertainty is, which is sourcing, not fabrication. Most schedules do
the opposite: they pad the build, which is the phase most under control, and leave sourcing
tight, which is the phase nobody controls.

The other lever is deciding early which elements are rented and which are made. Rental has a
different and usually shorter risk profile, and mixing the two deliberately is how a compressed
timeline gets met without cutting the design.
