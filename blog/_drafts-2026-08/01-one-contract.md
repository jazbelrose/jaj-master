# One contract, three disciplines

Most environments get built by three companies who have never worked together.

A designer draws the room. A fabricator builds to the drawing. An installer puts it in the
space. Each one bids separately, each one protects its own margin, and each one hands off to
the next with a set of documents and a hope. The client signs three contracts and becomes,
whether they wanted to or not, the general contractor.

Jensen and Juhl is one company doing all three. That is the whole proposition, and it is
worth being specific about what it changes, because "full service" is a phrase every vendor
uses and almost none of them mean.

## The drawing answers to the build

When the designer and the fabricator are different companies, the drawing is a negotiation.
The designer specifies what the room should be. The fabricator prices what it can build, and
the gap between those two things gets resolved by whoever has more leverage, usually late,
usually by cutting the thing that made the design worth doing.

When they are the same company, the constraint arrives while the drawing is still soft. The
person specifying a finish already knows what the shop can hold, what the material costs
this month, and how long treatment takes. The design that reaches the client is a design that
survives contact with the schedule, because it was built against the schedule from the start.

## Nobody bids the gap

Every handoff between companies has a seam, and every seam gets priced. The fabricator adds
contingency because the drawing might be wrong. The installer adds contingency because the
fabrication might be late. Neither of them is being unreasonable. They are pricing a risk
they cannot control.

One company holds that risk internally. It does not disappear, but it stops being marked up
three times on its way to the client.

## There is one number to call

The most expensive thing in production is not labor or material. It is the hour on site when
something does not fit and nobody present has authority to decide what happens next.

Under three contracts, that hour becomes an email chain. The installer calls the client, the
client calls the designer, the designer calls the fabricator, and the crew stands in the room
while it resolves. Under one contract, the person on site works for the same company as the
person who drew it. The decision happens in the room.

## What it costs us

Being honest about this matters more than the pitch. Self-performing means carrying things
between jobs that a broker does not carry. Shop space. Equipment. People on payroll in the
weeks when nothing is loading in. A brokered model is lighter and, in a slow quarter,
genuinely cheaper to run.

We carry it because the alternative is selling a room we cannot guarantee. A company that
subcontracts the build is, in the end, forwarding your job to someone you did not choose and
cannot call. When it goes well you never notice. When it goes badly you find out at load-in,
which is the one moment when nothing can be fixed.

## What this means for a buyer

Ask any vendor a simple question: who is actually building this, and who is actually
installing it? If the answer involves companies you have not met, you are not buying
execution. You are buying coordination, and you are the one doing it.
