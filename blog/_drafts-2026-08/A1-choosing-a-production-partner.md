# How to choose a production partner for a custom build

The short answer: find out who is actually doing the work, and find out before you sign.

Most of the risk in a custom environment is not design risk. It is coordination risk. The
room fails not because someone drew it wrong but because the drawing, the build and the
install lived in three different companies who each assumed someone else was handling the
part in between. Here is what to ask to find that out early, while you still have leverage.

## 1. Who fabricates this, and who installs it?

Ask it plainly and listen for names you have not heard. Plenty of production companies are
sales and design organizations that broker the build to a shop you will never meet and a
labor company you will never meet.

That is not automatically bad. Brokers can be excellent, and for some scopes it is the only
sensible structure. But you should know, because it determines who is standing in the room on
install day and whether that person can make a decision.

A useful follow-up: if something does not fit at load-in, who decides what happens, and do
they need to call anyone?

## 2. What happens when the drawing and the material disagree?

Every custom build has this moment. The specified finish is discontinued, the lead time moved,
the venue's dimensions were wrong on the plan.

What you want to hear is a process, not a reassurance. Who catches it, how fast you find out,
and who has authority to substitute. A partner who says "that does not happen to us" is
telling you they have not run enough jobs, or they are not going to tell you when it does.

## 3. What is the real lead time, and what drives it?

Not the schedule. The constraint behind the schedule.

In soft goods it is usually fabric availability and flame treatment, and treatment vendors
often quote a duration rather than a date, which means your calendar cannot be built backwards
from load-in until the material is actually in hand. In scenic it is shop capacity and truck
space. In both, the honest answer includes a thing that is outside their control.

If the lead time they give you has no dependencies in it, it is a sales number.

## 4. Who carries the risk if it is late or wrong?

Under a single contract this is simple. Under three, it is a question worth resolving in
writing before anyone starts, because the default answer is that you carry it.

Ask specifically: if fabrication runs late and the install window compresses, who pays for
the overtime crew? If it is not written down, it is you.

## 5. What do they own, and what do they rent?

This tells you more about a company than its portfolio. A shop with its own equipment and its
own crews has a different failure mode than one assembling a supply chain per job. Neither is
wrong, but the first can absorb a change and the second has to renegotiate it.

## What this looks like from our side

Jensen and Juhl operates as one house. Drapery Society fabricates soft goods, XCTN Production
builds scenic and lighting environments, and both run on crews and systems the house owns. We
answer these questions the same way every time because the answer does not change from job to
job.

That is not the only valid model. It is the one that made sense to us after enough jobs failed
at the seams between companies rather than inside any one of them.
