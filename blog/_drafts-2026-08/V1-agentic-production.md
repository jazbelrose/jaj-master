# Agentic systems are coming for production ops, not production

Nobody is going to automate sewing a sixteen-foot velvet panel, or flying truss in a room with
a bad ceiling, or reading a venue and knowing the drawing is wrong before the tape comes out.
That work is physical, it is judgment, and it is not going anywhere.

What is going somewhere is everything wrapped around it. The estimate rebuilt from scratch for
the fourth time. The crew call confirmed by text message. The invoice reconciled against a
budget that changed twice since it was approved. The purchase order that exists only in
somebody's sent folder. That layer is most of the hours in a production company, almost none
of the value, and it is exactly what agentic systems are good at.

## The industry's actual software problem

Production runs on spreadsheets and group chats. Not because the people are unsophisticated,
but because the available software was built for other industries and does not model this one.

Project tools assume a task is the unit of work. In production, the unit is a person committed
for a duration: a crew member is on a job for four days, not assigned to seventeen discrete
tickets. Accounting tools assume an invoice follows a completed deliverable. In production the
money moves before the material does, because you buy fabric months before anyone sets foot in
a room.

When the tool does not fit the shape of the work, people route around it, and the routing
around is the spreadsheet.

## What an agentic system actually does here

Not "AI designs your event." That framing is a marketing fiction and everyone in the field
knows it.

The real version is narrower and more valuable. A system that watches the operational surface
and acts on it:

- Reads the budget, notices a line has drifted from the approved revision, and says so before
  the invoice is cut rather than after.
- Watches the inbox for the vendor quote that has not come back and follows up without anyone
  remembering to.
- Knows a treatment vendor answers with a duration instead of a date, and flags the schedule
  built backwards from a date that does not exist yet.
- Holds the whole history of a job so the answer to "what did we quote them in June" takes a
  second instead of an afternoon.

None of that is creative. All of it is the difference between a shop that scales and one that
does not.

## Why we built it ourselves

We did not set out to be a software company. We built memry because we ran out of ways to make
existing tools describe how a production house actually works, and because the operational
knowledge worth encoding is the knowledge you only get from running the jobs.

That is the part that does not transfer. A software company can build a better interface. It
cannot know that a week from a rental house often bills as three days, or which fire standard a
rigid substrate actually certifies to, or that the design you hand over before a contract is
signed is the design a competitor will build. Those are scars, and they are what makes an
operating system for this industry correct rather than merely functional.

## Where this goes

The production companies that survive the next decade will not be the ones with the best
renders. Rendering is already commoditized and getting more so. They will be the ones whose
operations are legible enough to scale, because the constraint on a shop like ours has never
been talent or demand. It is how many jobs you can hold in your head at once.

Agentic systems raise that number. That is the whole thesis, and it is less romantic than the
version being sold at conferences.
